Chauncey, Ohio has a long history with water. The village sits near the confluence of Sunday Creek and the Hocking River, and it has long been vulnerable to flooding. Crews serving ZIP code 45719 handle creek-driven water, wet basements and plumbing failures with the same process: stop the source, extract, dry and repair.

Creek water and the Hocking River

Flood damage here is different from a pipe leak. Creek water carries silt and contaminants, so porous materials such as carpet, pad and insulation are usually removed. Hard surfaces can be cleaned and dried. The flood damage page explains the category rules.

If water is rising, move to higher ground first. Do not drive through flooded roads, and do not enter a basement with standing water near the electrical panel. Call the 24/7 line once you are safe.

What is specific to Chauncey

Chauncey was laid out in 1839 and named for Elihu Chauncey, a partner in a firm that owned several salt springs nearby. A post office has operated here since 1838. Deep coal mining along Sunday Creek took over the local economy by the late nineteenth century.

Because of repeated flooding, low-lying land in the village was converted into green space. The Chauncey-Dover Community Park covers 127 acres of former strip-mine land, and the Chauncey Canal Trail and Wetland Preserve covers 13.5 acres along Sunday Creek. The Hocking River Paddle Route starts here.

State Route 682 ends at a junction with State Route 13. After US 33 was rerouted in the 1960s, SR 682 was extended to keep direct access to Athens. The village is at an elevation of about 659 feet and is in Dover Township. The Athens County Infirmary just outside the village is on the National Register of Historic Places.

Source type: Public reference (Wikipedia), Athens Conservancy and park descriptions

Housing in a mining and canal town

Older homes in a village like Chauncey often have stone or block foundations, crawl spaces and original plumbing. If your house is older, check the plumbing for galvanized or cast iron runs, which corrode from the inside. A crawl space that collects creek-fed groundwater needs pumping and drying, and the crawl space page explains how.

If water is still coming in

Take these steps while you wait for a crew.

1

Get to higher ground

If creek water is rising toward the house, leave. Property can be restored. People cannot.

2

Stay out of basements with water

Water can hide electrical hazards. Wait for the utility if the panel is wet.

3

Shut off the main valve

If the water is from a pipe, close the main to stop it.

4

Photograph the water line

Take photos of the depth and the damage before anything is moved.

Call the 24/7 line

What crews do in Chauncey homes

The sequence is the same as elsewhere: extract the water, remove what cannot be saved, dry the structure and repair. The basement cleanup page covers the below-grade room, and the sewage page covers backups.

Creek flooding often means a sewer or septic system is also overloaded, so ask the crew to check for contaminated water before anyone enters.

Dry weather does not end the risk

After the water recedes, the structure stays wet for days. Wall cavities, subfloors and insulation hold moisture. Skipping the dry-out leaves odor and swollen materials behind. The structural drying page explains how readings show when a house is dry.

Frequently asked questions

Do you cover every ZIP code in Chauncey?

Chauncey is served as ZIP code 45719. For addresses just outside the village, call and give the street address so crews can confirm coverage.

Is emergency water removal available in Chauncey?

Yes. The 24/7 line is answered at night and on weekends, and same-day response is offered when crews are available.

Is Chauncey in a flood zone?

Parts of the village near Sunday Creek and the Hocking River are low-lying. Check the FEMA flood map for your exact address, and ask your insurer about flood coverage.

Will homeowners insurance cover creek flooding?

Standard homeowners policies typically do not cover flood. Ask your insurer, and ask about flood insurance if you have not already.

Should I wait for the water to go down before calling?

No. Call now so crews can plan the dry-out, and follow safety steps while the water recedes.
